# How one email locks a business

A common attack called **ransomware**:

- An employee gets an email: *"Your bill is overdue, open the attached invoice."*
- They open the attachment. It quietly encrypts the company's files and waits.
- One morning: *"Your files are locked. Pay to get them back."* Sales records, staff details, supplier list, all unreadable.

**No lock was picked. No window was broken. One email, one click.** Something valuable, a way in, a weakness, and harm: that is the whole subject in one picture.

---

<!-- 1.3 -->

# What cybersecurity is

**Cybersecurity is protecting information and the systems that hold it from people who should not read it, change it, or stop it from working.**

- **Information:** records, a phone number, a password, a bank balance.
- **Systems:** laptops, phones, servers, apps, websites, and the networks between them.
- **People who should not:** from a curious insider to a criminal who locks files for money.

It is not one product you buy. It is an ongoing practice: understand what could go wrong, protect what matters, and watch for what gets through.

---

<!-- 1.4 -->
<!-- layout: table -->

# Why it matters now

Almost everything runs on computers and networks, and an attack can reach it from anywhere in the world.

| What depends on computers | What an attack can do |
|---|---|
| A business's records and payroll | Lock the files; stop the business |
| A person's phone | Read private chats; steal from a banking app |
| A bank or payment app | Move money; take over accounts |
| A hospital's records | Block access during an emergency |

In Cambodia, as online banking and QR payments spread, scams and account takeovers grow with them. Security is for every business and every phone, not only big companies.

---

<!-- 1.5 -->

# Risk, not perfection

No system is ever perfectly safe, and trying to make one perfect costs more than it is worth.

Security asks four questions about anything that could go wrong:

- What could happen?
- How likely is it?
- How bad would it be?
- What does it cost to prevent?

A coffee shop needs less protection than a bank. **Neither needs zero.**

---

<!-- 1.6 -->
<!-- layout: recap -->

# Recap: what security is

- One email and one click can lock a whole business: that is the harm security prevents.
- Cybersecurity protects **information and systems** from people who should not read, change, or stop them.
- Everything runs on computers now, so the harm can reach anyone, anywhere.
- Security is about **risk**, not perfection.

# Assets: what is worth protecting

An **asset** is anything that has value to you, and so might have value to an attacker. You defend what matters most, first.

| Asset (a dental clinic) | Why it has value |
|---|---|
| Patient records | Private and legally protected |
| The appointment system | Down = no patients seen |
| Payment and banking details | Direct access to money |
| The clinic's reputation | Patients who feel unsafe leave |

**Trust and reputation are assets too.** You can restore files from a backup in an hour; winning back a patient's trust takes much longer.

---

<!-- 2.3 -->

# The three questions

Look at any system and ask three things. The next lesson studies them as the **CIA triad**.

1. **Can the wrong people _read_ it?** → confidentiality (only you and your bank read your balance)
2. **Can the wrong people _change_ it?** → integrity (nobody changes an order from $100 to $1)
3. **Can the wrong people _stop_ it?** → availability (hospital records open in an emergency)

Ransomware is a **stop** attack: the files are not read or changed, just made unusable when they are needed.

---

<!-- 2.4 -->

# Threat, vulnerability, risk

Three words people mix up. They are different things.

- **Threat:** a bad thing that could happen. *"A criminal could lock our files for money."*
- **Vulnerability:** a weakness that lets it happen. *"Staff open attachments without checking."*
- **Risk:** the chance of the two meeting, and how bad it would be.

> **Risk = how likely × how bad.**

A threat with no matching weakness is little risk. Security work is finding the risks that are both **likely** and **serious**, and reducing them first.

---

<!-- 2.5 -->
<!-- layout: recap -->

# Recap: what we protect

- **Assets** are what is worth protecting, including trust and reputation.
- The three questions: can the wrong people **read**, **change**, or **stop** it?
- A **threat** is the bad event, a **vulnerability** is the weakness, a **risk** is the chance and cost of the two meeting.

# Defense in depth

The ransomware did not have to win. Five ordinary layers:

1. **Training:** staff pause at a strange invoice email.
2. **An email filter:** the message goes to junk.
3. **Up-to-date software:** the attachment's trick no longer works.
4. **A separate daily account:** the locking program cannot reach every file.
5. **A nightly backup, kept disconnected:** files restored in an hour.

The attacker must beat **all five**. Miss the filter? Training stops the click. Click anyway? The backup saves the day.

**Every layer can fail, so we never rely on one.**

---

<!-- 3.3 -->
<!-- layout: table -->

# Security is everyone's job

The weak point in a ransomware attack is rarely a machine. It is an ordinary email and an ordinary click.

| Who | Their part |
|---|---|
| The owner / manager | Decides what matters; pays for backups and training |
| The staff | Spot strange emails; use good passwords |
| The developer | Builds software that does not leak or over-trust |
| The server / IT | Checks every request; owns the decisions |
| The specialist | Finds weaknesses; watches and responds |

The danger: each part assuming another has it covered. *"We have antivirus, so we don't need backups."*

---

<!-- 3.4 -->

# Why we learn to attack

This program teaches real attacks, in a safe lab. Why, if the goal is to defend?

**Because you cannot defend against what you do not understand.**

If you have never seen how a file is locked or a password is cracked, your defenses are guesses.

- A **locksmith** must understand lock-picking to fit good locks.
- The skill is the same; **permission and intent** decide right from wrong.
- Hired to pick your lock: a professional. The same act on a stranger's door: a burglar.

---

<!-- 3.5 -->
<!-- layout: warning -->

# The one rule

> **Only ever test systems that you own, or that you have clear, written permission to test.**

Every tool here is powerful. On your own lab, it is a skill that pays well. On someone else's system without permission, the same action is a crime.

- "I was only curious" is **not** permission.
- "It was easy to break into" is **not** permission.
- "I only looked, I didn't change anything" is **not** permission.

Your own lab, or written permission. There is no third option.
