## Video 1 — The Program, and the Foundations

### 1.1 — Title

**Show:** slide 1.1

**Say:** This is the final lesson of Security Fundamentals. It doesn't add new theory; it turns everything you've learned into a practical plan. We'll look at how the whole program is organized, why the Foundations come first, and how to choose a path.

**Time:** about 0:25

### 1.2 — How the program is organized

**Show:** slide 1.2

**Say:** The program is built as a path from knowing nothing to being ready for professional work, in five stages, in a deliberate order. Foundations: the ground every security person stands on. Offensive: how attacks really work, hands-on. Defensive: how to watch, catch, and recover. Engineering: how to build systems that resist attack. And Specialized: deep fields, and turning skills into a job. The order isn't arbitrary, each stage depends on the ones before it. You can't meaningfully attack or defend a network you don't understand, and you can't understand a network without the Foundations. So the single most important rule of the roadmap is this: do the Foundations first, in order, and don't rush them.

**Time:** about 0:50

### 1.3 — The Foundations, and why they come first

**Show:** slide 1.3

**Say:** It's tempting to jump straight to the exciting courses, "I want to hack things now". Resist it. Almost everyone who struggles later skipped or skimmed the Foundations, and almost everyone who progresses smoothly built them solidly. How Computers Work, because you can't analyze what a program does if you don't know what a program is. Linux, the native environment of security tools. Networking, the language of nearly every attack and defense. Windows and Active Directory, what most organizations run and most attacks target. Scripting and Python, to automate work and build your own tools. The home lab, your safe, legal place to practise. And cryptography, the math behind passwords, HTTPS, and signatures, understood, not memorized. Think of the Foundations as the alphabet and grammar before writing essays. Give them the time they deserve.

**Time:** about 0:55

### 1.4 — Choosing a path

**Show:** slide 1.4

**Say:** Once the Foundations are solid, you specialize, and the three main paths match the role families from last lesson. Offensive, if you love figuring out how things break. Defensive, if you like watching, investigating, noticing patterns, and staying calm under pressure, and this path has the most jobs and is a common, reliable way in. And Engineering, if you like building, automating, and designing systems well. You don't have to choose forever, or choose only one; a sensible approach is to do the path that excites you most first, then broaden, because understanding more than one side makes you more valuable. And if you're unsure, start Defensive: it has the broadest job market, teaches you to think about attacks from the receiving end, and gives a strong base to pivot from.

**Time:** about 0:50

### 1.5 — Recap

**Show:** slide 1.5

**Say:** So: five stages, Foundations to a path to Specialized, each depending on the last. Do the Foundations first, in order, they're the real base. And pick the path that excites you, or start Defensive if you're unsure. Next, the habits that actually build skill: practising, and a portfolio.

**Time:** about 0:25

## Video 2 — Practising, a Portfolio, and Your Next Step

### 2.1 — Title

**Show:** slide 2.1

**Say:** Knowing the roadmap isn't enough; you have to walk it. In this final video: learning by doing in a home lab, practising with CTFs, building a portfolio, and your concrete next step.

**Time:** about 0:25

### 2.2 — Learn by doing: the home lab

**Show:** slide 2.2

**Say:** Here's the single most important study habit in this whole field: you cannot learn security by reading alone. Reading about lock-picking doesn't make you a locksmith. You need hands-on practice in a safe, legal environment, and that's your home lab, which you'll build in the Home Lab course. It's a set of virtual machines on your own computer: an attacker machine, some deliberately vulnerable targets, and an isolated network. Everything belongs to you, so the ethics rule is satisfied by design, you can attack and defend freely and legally. Use it well: do, don't just watch, performing every technique yourself; break things and then fix them, because watching both sides teaches far more than either alone; take notes as you go, because they become your portfolio; and use snapshots to reset and repeat.

**Time:** about 0:55

### 2.3 — Practise: CTFs and challenges

**Show:** slide 2.3

**Say:** Beyond your own lab, the community runs a huge amount of free, legal practice. CTFs, Capture The Flag, are competitions where you solve challenges by finding a hidden flag, in categories like web, cryptography, forensics, reverse engineering, and binary exploitation, and the final course of this program is devoted to them. Practice platforms host deliberately vulnerable machines you can work through at your own pace, legally, because they exist for exactly this purpose. Why practise this way? It's legal, it's graded so you know when you succeed, it builds real problem-solving stamina, and it connects you to a community. And it compounds: even 30 minutes a few times a week turns into serious skill over months.

**Time:** about 0:50

### 2.4 — Build a portfolio

**Show:** slide 2.4

**Say:** Remember from last lesson: skills and a portfolio get you hired. A portfolio is simply evidence of what you can do, and you can start building it now, long before your first job, which matters especially when you lack formal experience. Good pieces: write-ups, clear explanations of how you solved a challenge, what the weakness was, and how to fix it, which show both skill and communication; a blog or notes site, because teaching something proves you understand it; small security tools or scripts you've written; and documented lab work. Two tips: keep it honest, never claim work you didn't do, because integrity is everything in this field; and keep it clear, a well-explained simple project beats a vague complex one. Start the write-up habit with your very next challenge or lab.

**Time:** about 0:50

### 2.5 — Your next step

**Show:** slide 2.5

**Say:** So let's bring it home. Learn by doing: build the home lab and practise with CTFs. Build a portfolio of honest, clear write-ups and projects. And stay current and stay ethical, because as your skills grow, so does your power to cause harm, and the professionals with long, respected careers are the ones who never cross that line. Your reputation for integrity is the most valuable thing you'll build. Your next step is simple: start the next Foundation course, How Computers Work, and continue the Foundations in order; plan to build your home lab; start keeping notes from today; and decide, loosely, which path calls to you. Security rewards curiosity, persistence, and integrity more than raw talent, and demand far exceeds supply. You've taken the first step. Keep going, and welcome to the field.

**Time:** about 0:55
